\chapter{Biased Beliefs}
\label{ch:beliefs}

The benchmark model of investor beliefs is Bayes' rule. Agents hold prior probabilities over states of the world, observe signals whose likelihoods they know, and update to posterior probabilities by Bayes' formula. Combined with the expected-utility benchmark of Chapter~\ref{ch:preferences}, rational Bayes--expected-utility agents trading in frictionless markets deliver the predictions reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}---prices track rational forecasts of cash flows, expected returns in the cross-section line up with risk, and investors hold diversified portfolios and trade only on information. Like the expected-utility benchmark, the Bayes benchmark is the wrong descriptor of how investors actually form beliefs, and the descriptive gaps are large enough to matter for prices. Laboratory experiments, survey data, and field evidence document systematic departures from Bayes' rule---representativeness, base-rate neglect, sample-size neglect, overconfidence, over-extrapolation, conservatism, anchoring, belief perseverance---and these departures show up in trading behavior, in expectations surveys, and in the cross-sectional and time-series properties of asset returns.

This chapter develops that evidence. The first half (Sections~\ref{sec:representativeness}--\ref{sec:otherbiases}) is the \emph{psychology of beliefs}: a survey of the cognitive biases that distort investors' probability assessments, together with the asset pricing applications each bias has been claimed to explain. The second half (Sections~\ref{sec:hetsources}--\ref{sec:causal}) is the \emph{heterogeneous beliefs} literature: rather than assuming a single representative investor with biased beliefs, the second half dispenses with the representative assumption and asks what cross-sectional variation in beliefs does to prices---especially when combined with short-sale constraints. Section~\ref{sec:stakes} asks whether people ever learn, Section~\ref{sec:beliefscontested} catalogues the limits of the biased-beliefs program, and Section~\ref{sec:beliefsahead} closes the chapter.

\section{Representativeness}
\label{sec:representativeness}

\begin{definition}[Representativeness heuristic]
\label{def:representativeness}
A person judging the probability that object $A$ belongs to class $B$, or that event $A$ was generated by process $B$, evaluates the probability by the degree to which $A$ resembles $B$---the \emph{degree to which $A$ reflects the essential characteristics of $B$} \citep{kahneman1974}.
\end{definition}

The representativeness heuristic is useful in many settings: the prediction that a small, dense, red fruit is more likely an apple than a cherry is exactly the kind of inference the heuristic supports. The bias enters when the resemblance judgment is made \emph{without} taking into account base rates and sample sizes, the two pieces of statistical information a Bayesian calculation would weight heavily. \citet{kahneman1974} document two robust violations.

\subsection{Base-Rate Neglect}

The standard demonstration is the ``Steve'' problem. Subjects read a personality sketch of a fictional person---shy, withdrawn, helpful, neat, with little interest in people---and are asked whether Steve is more likely a librarian or a farmer. The modal answer is that Steve is a librarian, even though the base rates in the U.S.\ population differ by a factor of more than 20: there are roughly 20 times more farmers than librarians. The vivid description is treated as evidence about Steve's occupation and the base-rate evidence is largely ignored. A Bayesian agent would combine the description with the base rates, and the base rates would dominate; a representativeness judge treats only the description.

\subsection{Sample-Size Neglect}

The second violation is the failure to weight evidence by the sample size from which it is drawn. A sequence of three heads from a fair coin is taken as strong evidence that the coin is biased toward heads, even though the posterior probability of bias after three flips is still close to the prior. The same judgment applied to a sequence of thirty heads would be more defensible. \citet{gilovich1985} document the consequence in basketball: fans and players judge a player who has made a few shots in a row to be ``hot,'' and adjust their expectations of his next shot upward. The empirical evidence in large samples is that the hot hand is largely a myth---shot-level success rates are essentially uncorrelated with the recent history of makes and misses. \citet{gilovich1985} read the phenomenon as representativeness: fans expect a short streak of makes to be representative of a player's true ability, and they expect the next shot to reflect that true ability. In finance, the same bias is the source of over-extrapolation, to which Section~\ref{sec:overextrapolation} returns.

\section{Overconfidence}
\label{sec:overconfidence}

\begin{definition}[Overconfidence]
\label{def:overconf}
Overconfidence is the tendency to believe one's judgments are more accurate than they actually are. The literature distinguishes \emph{overprecision} (confidence in the accuracy of one's forecasts), \emph{overplacement} (belief that one is better than others), and \emph{overestimation} (unrealistically positive self-assessments of ability).
\end{definition}

The cleanest laboratory evidence is on overprecision. \citet{alpert1982} asked subjects to give 98\% confidence intervals for unknown quantities (the area of Manhattan, the number of piano tuners in Chicago). Across many studies, the realized coverage rate of these intervals is closer to 60\% than 98\%. Subjects are wildly miscalibrated: the ranges they assign are too narrow for the information they have.

\subsection{CFOs and the Stock Market}

The overprecision finding extends to professional investors. \citet{bendavid2013} surveyed chief financial officers of U.S.\ corporations and asked them to report probability distributions for the one-year-ahead return on the S\&P~500. The CFOs' distributions are very tight: their 80\% confidence intervals span about 28 percentage points of expected return, centered on a relatively narrow range of point estimates. The realized market return, over the same horizon, falls inside the CFOs' 80\% interval only about 36\% of the time. The professional respondents are not only miscalibrated, they are miscalibrated in the same direction as the student subjects of \citet{alpert1982}: their intervals are too narrow by a factor of two or more.

\subsection{Overplacement}

A separate strand of evidence concerns \emph{overplacement}---the belief that one is above average. \citet{svenson1981} surveyed drivers in the U.S.\ and Sweden and found that 93\% of American respondents (and a large majority of Swedish respondents) rate themselves as above-average drivers. The figure is mechanically impossible: the average driver is, by definition, average. The same pattern appears in finance: in surveys of investor confidence, retail investors systematically report that their own portfolio will outperform the average portfolio of their peers.

\subsection{Excessive Trading}

Overconfidence has direct trading implications. \citet{odean1999} showed that the average household in a large discount-brokerage sample turns over roughly 80\% of its portfolio each year and that net returns fall sharply with turnover: the gross return on the portfolio is roughly uncorrelated with trading intensity, but trading costs---commissions and bid--ask spreads---rise with it. The households that trade most earn less than the households that trade least, and the average household earns less than a passive index fund. \citet{hong2007} document the matching aggregate fact that trading volume is far in excess of what any rational model with symmetric information can generate.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{slide08_pic6.png}
\caption[Trading volume and disagreement]{\textbf{Excessive trading.} The figure shows that turnover in equity markets is far higher than would be implied by liquidity-motivated or rebalancing trades alone, and that the volume is concentrated in periods when investor beliefs diverge. The dispersion in beliefs is the natural reading of the volume pattern. \textit{Source:} EF8083 course materials, after \citet{hong2007}.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Gender Differences}

\citet{barber2001} show that the overconfidence bias is not gender-neutral. In their brokerage dataset, men trade roughly 1.5 times as often as women and earn correspondingly lower net returns; the gender gap in net returns is largest among singles and shrinks (but does not disappear) among married households, when household portfolios are pooled. \citet{lundeberg1994} document the matching psychological result: in laboratory studies of confidence judgments, men report higher confidence than women on tasks of comparable difficulty, with the gap widening on tasks at which both perform poorly. The two strands of evidence are mutually reinforcing: the gender gap in trading costs is consistent with the gender gap in confidence, and the mechanism operates through over-trading rather than through any systematic gender difference in stock-selection skill.

\subsection{Momentum and Overconfidence}

\citet{daniel1998} develop a formal model in which overconfidence interacts with \emph{self-attribution bias} to produce momentum. Investors are overconfident about private signals they themselves generate (e.g., their own research) but less so about public signals. Positive returns reinforce their overconfidence through self-attribution---a streak of good calls is attributed to skill, a streak of bad calls to bad luck---and they continue to trade on their private signals with growing positions. Eventually, the public information that contradicts their private view arrives, and prices partially correct. The dynamics produce short-horizon continuation (momentum) followed by partial reversal as information is revealed.

The model predicts that momentum should be stronger in environments where overconfidence is culturally or institutionally more pronounced. \citet{chui2010} test this prediction using Hofstede's individualism index as a proxy for cultural variation in overconfidence. Individualism, in Hofstede's framework, captures the extent to which a society rewards individual initiative and self-promotion relative to group cohesion---a pattern that maps naturally onto overplacement. The data confirm the prediction: momentum profits are reliably larger in more individualistic countries, both at the country level and for individual firms operating in more individualistic environments.

\caption[Momentum and individualism]{\textbf{Momentum and individualism.} The figure shows momentum profits sorted by Hofstede individualism scores across countries. Momentum is stronger in more individualistic societies, consistent with the overconfidence reading of the \citet{daniel1998} model. \textit{Source:} \citet{chui2010}, reproduced from the original article.}

\section{Over-Extrapolation}
\label{sec:overextrapolation}

\begin{definition}[Over-extrapolation]
\label{def:overextrapolation}
The estimate of the future value of a quantity is a positive function of the recent past values of that quantity. The mechanism is the representativeness heuristic: a recent history of, say, increasing prices is judged representative of a generally increasing trend, so the next observation is expected to be larger still.
\end{definition}

Over-extrapolation is the most widely applied idea in behavioral finance. It is the source of return extrapolation (predicting future returns from past returns), of fundamentals extrapolation (predicting future earnings from past growth), and, in aggregate, of the survey-implied ``expected returns'' that move with valuation ratios in ways rational discount-rate models cannot easily reproduce. The applications span almost every asset pricing puzzle of the past twenty years.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{13.png}
\caption[The over-extrapolation feedback loop]{\textbf{Over-extrapolation in pictures.} Good news lifts recent observations, which are extrapolated to lift beliefs about future values; the higher beliefs in turn push prices further up; the price rise confirms the recent observations. The loop runs until some shock breaks it. \textit{Source:} EF8083 course materials.}
\label{fig:loop}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Survey Evidence on Extrapolation}

\citet{greenwood2014} assemble survey expectations of one-year-ahead stock market returns from six sources, including the Gallup poll, the University of Michigan's survey of consumer sentiment, the Survey of Professional Forecasters, and several investor surveys. They compare the survey-implied expected return to realized subsequent returns over the same horizon and find a strongly negative correlation: the higher investors' expected returns, the lower realized returns are subsequently. The sign is the central fact of the paper. Survey expectations rise after market run-ups---they extrapolate recent returns---and high expectations therefore coincide with the conditions that precede low subsequent returns, the opposite of what rational time-varying-discount-rate models imply for measured expected returns. The authors then connect survey expectations to investor flows into equity mutual funds and show that the flows chase the same states of the world: inflows rise when expected returns are high, exactly the pattern that produces overvaluation when sentiment reverses.

The pattern is consistent with extrapolation: when recent returns are high, expected returns are also high, and subsequent returns are low---the expectations are inverted by the next period, not vindicated. \textit{Source:} \citet{greenwood2014}.}

\subsection{Measuring the Degree of Extrapolation: DOX}

\citet{cassella2018} propose a formal measure of the weight investors place on recent returns. Their measure, called \emph{DOX} for degree of extrapolation, is the complement of a parameter $\lambda$ in a forecaster's representation of next-period expected return as a discounted weighted average of past returns. Lower $\lambda$ implies faster forgetting of past observations and heavier weight on the most recent return.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{slide16_pic4.png}
\hspace{0.3em}
\includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{slide16_pic5.png}
\caption[DOX: degree of extrapolation]{\textbf{Degree of extrapolation.} The two panels show the time series of the extrapolation parameter estimated from survey data, together with its correlation with subsequent realized market returns. Higher DOX (lower $\lambda$) corresponds to investors placing heavier weight on recent observations. \textit{Source:} \citet{cassella2018}.}
\label{fig:dox}
\end{figure}

DOX enters asset prices through the predictability of returns by price-scaled fundamentals (dividend yield, book-to-market, earnings-to-price). When DOX is high, investors quickly forget the price changes that drove valuations up; they remain willing to extrapolate from the most recent observation; and high valuation ratios carry less information about low future returns. When DOX is low, investors remember past prices more completely; high valuation ratios are more informative about low future returns. The DOX measure is therefore not just a description of beliefs but a state variable that conditions the predictability of returns---and \citet{cassella2018} document that the conditional predictability is present in the data.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{slide17_pic4.png}
\caption[Predictability of returns conditional on DOX]{\textbf{DOX and predictability.} The figure plots the slope from predictive regressions of subsequent returns on the price--dividend ratio, conditional on the estimated DOX measure. The slope is steeper (more predictability) when DOX is low: investors remember the past better, and high valuations more strongly forecast low future returns. \textit{Source:} \citet{cassella2018}.}
\label{fig:dox_cond}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Fundamentals Extrapolation}

A parallel stream of evidence concerns the extrapolation of fundamentals rather than prices. \citet{bordalo2023} show that long-term growth (LTG) expectations of corporate earnings overreact to good and bad news: a positive earnings surprise shifts the distribution of LTG forecasts upward by more than is justified by the signal alone. The authors track an aggregate LTG-expectation measure over time and show that its deviations from a fundamental benchmark are highly correlated with the price--dividend ratio and predict low subsequent market returns over a five-year horizon. The mechanism is the same as in return extrapolation: investors apply a diagnostic forecasting rule that overweights representative recent observations, and the implied LTG co-moves with prices and reverts.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{slide18_pic3.png}
\caption[LTG expectations, the price--dividend ratio, and returns]{\textbf{Fundamentals extrapolation.} The figure overlays a measure of long-term earnings-growth (LTG) expectations (red) with the price--dividend ratio (green) and five-year-ahead realized stock returns (black). LTG expectations track the price--dividend ratio and predict low subsequent returns, consistent with extrapolative beliefs. \textit{Source:} \citet{bordalo2023}.}
\label{fig:ltg}
\end{figure}

\section{Other Biases: Conservatism, Anchoring, and Feelings}
\label{sec:otherbiases}

The biases catalogued so far all push investors to \emph{overreact} to new information---to be more confident in their forecasts, more certain about the trend, and more aggressive in their trading. The literature also identifies biases that push in the opposite direction, underreacting to news.

\subsection{Conservatism}

\begin{definition}[Conservatism]
\label{def:conservatism}
\emph{Conservatism} is the tendency to update beliefs too slowly in response to new information \citep{edwards1968}. In the canonical Bayesian account, agents place posterior probability somewhere between the prior and the signal-consistent likelihood; the conservatism bias is the finding that the updating weight on the signal is smaller than the Bayesian formula implies.
\end{definition}

The conservatism bias is the natural complement to over-extrapolation. Conservatism by itself would predict slow price adjustment to news and the post-earnings-announcement drift of Section~\ref{sec:pead}; over-extrapolation by itself would predict sharp initial reaction followed by reversal. \citet{barberis1998} build a model in which conservatism at short horizons and representativeness at long horizons combine to produce exactly the joint pattern of momentum and long-term reversal that the data show.

\subsection{Belief Perseverance and Confirmation Bias}

Two related biases affect how investors \emph{search} for and \emph{interpret} evidence. \emph{Belief perseverance} is the tendency, once an opinion is formed, to be slow to revise it on receipt of new evidence; subjects often continue to endorse a belief even after the evidence that produced it has been fully discredited. \emph{Confirmation bias} is the active counterpart: investors seek out evidence that supports their prior and interpret ambiguous evidence as supportive. The two effects compound: investors both fail to look for falsifying evidence and discount it when they encounter it. \citet{stanovich2013} show that higher cognitive ability can amplify confirmation bias, because more capable reasoners are better at constructing arguments for their existing position.

\subsection{Anchoring}

\begin{definition}[Anchoring]
\label{def:anchoring}
\emph{Anchoring} is the tendency, in forming numerical estimates of uncertain quantities, to adjust insufficiently from some initial value (the ``anchor''), even when the anchor is generated randomly and is clearly irrelevant \citep{kahneman1974}.
\end{definition}

In the canonical demonstration, \citet{kahneman1974} spin a wheel of fortune in front of subjects to produce a random number, ask subjects whether the percentage of African nations in the United Nations is greater or less than that number, and then ask them to estimate the actual percentage. Estimates of the latter are systematically shifted toward the random anchor. The financial applications include IPO pricing (where the initial price range functions as an anchor for analysts), analyst price targets, and merger valuations (where the announced offer price anchors subsequent negotiation).

\subsection{The Effect of Feelings}

A more recent strand of research investigates how feelings affect beliefs and preferences. \emph{Affective feelings}---mood and emotion---shift judgments in ways unrelated to the evidence: a happier mood produces more optimistic assessments, a sadder mood more pessimistic. \citet{edmans2006} document the case in finance: when the national soccer team loses a World Cup elimination match, the national stock market falls significantly the next trading day, with the effect concentrated in countries where soccer is more important. The economic mechanism is not information about fundamentals but mood: disappointing sports news worsens the national mood and depresses risk-taking.

\citet{wang2020} extend the argument to a starker exogenous shock. Terrorist attacks raise aggregate risk aversion, as proxied by flows out of equity mutual funds and into government bond funds. The effect is larger in the days immediately following the attack and decays over time. Both findings point to a direct channel from affective feelings to beliefs about risk and, through beliefs, to portfolio choices.

\emph{Cognitive feelings} are about the ease with which information is processed rather than the mood that processing leaves behind. \citet{zajonc1968} and \citet{bornstein1989} document the basic result: people prefer stimuli that are familiar, easily processed, and fluently retrieved, even when the preference has no informational basis. \citet{uppal2003} formalize the asset pricing version: under ambiguity aversion or model misspecification, investors view stocks whose payoffs they can better estimate as less ambiguous, and overweight them. The applications are \emph{home bias} (domestic stocks are more familiar than foreign stocks) and \emph{local bias} (stocks headquartered nearby are more familiar than distant ones), both of which Chapter~\ref{ch:intro} documents.

\section{Heterogeneous Beliefs: Sources}
\label{sec:hetsources}

The biases of Sections~\ref{sec:representativeness}--\ref{sec:otherbiases} all keep the \emph{representative investor} assumption intact: a single agent, possibly with systematically distorted beliefs, faces the asset pricing problem. The next two sections drop that assumption and work with heterogeneous beliefs---different agents holding different views about asset values, with the average belief unbiased. The departure is consequential. Asymmetric information alone cannot sustain rational disagreement: under common priors, a Bayesian agent who is offered a trade recognizes that the offer itself reflects information he does not have, and revises his beliefs until the apparent gains from trade vanish. The \citet{milgrom1981} no-trade theorem formalizes the result: with common priors and rational updating, agents who observe the same signals agree to agree, and no trade takes place. Heterogeneous beliefs in the rational sense must therefore come from somewhere beyond asymmetric information. The literature has identified three sources.

\subsection{Experience Effects}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{slide30_pic3.jpg}
\caption[Personal experience and beliefs]{\textbf{Experience effects.} The figure illustrates how personal experience shapes beliefs: people place disproportionate weight on events they have lived through and form reference points from those events. The same statistical evidence can produce different posterior beliefs for agents with different life histories. \textit{Source:} EF8083 course materials.}
\label{fig:experience}
\end{figure}

\citet{malmendier2016} document a clean case in inflation expectations. Survey respondents overweight the inflation they experienced during their own lifetimes when forming expectations of future inflation: respondents born in the 1930s report higher inflation expectations in 2010 than respondents born in the 1970s, even though the cohort born later has experienced more inflation in absolute terms. Younger respondents update their beliefs more quickly in response to recent news; older respondents update more slowly and rely more heavily on their formative experiences. The same mechanism---overweighting personally experienced events---produces persistent cross-sectional dispersion in beliefs about any quantity for which the historical record is long.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{slide31_pic3.png}
\caption[Inflation expectations and lived experience]{\textbf{Inflation expectations.} Survey respondents' expectations of long-run inflation overweight the inflation experienced during their own lifetimes. The cohort born in the 1930s reports higher expected inflation in 2010 than the cohort born in the 1970s, opposite to what the realized record implies. \textit{Source:} \citet{malmendier2016}.}
\label{fig:malmendier}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Political Polarization}

\citet{goldman2025} document a more recent source of belief dispersion in financial news. The authors compare the same corporate financial news covered by the conservative \emph{Wall Street Journal} and the liberal \emph{New York Times}. The two newspapers use systematically more positive language for firms aligned with their political leanings, and the gap is wider for politically sensitive topics (climate, labor, executive compensation) than for neutral ones. Investors who consume one source rather than the other form different beliefs about the same companies, and the dispersion persists because the consumers do not switch sources. The finding is part of a broader pattern: media fragmentation along political lines is a quantitatively significant source of disagreement about financial facts.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{slide32_pic4.png}
\caption[Political polarization in financial news]{\textbf{Political polarization in financial news.} Tone-of-language scores for corporate financial coverage by the \emph{Wall Street Journal} (conservative) and \emph{New York Times} (liberal), separated by firm-level political alignment. Both newspapers use systematically more positive language for firms aligned with their political leanings, with the gap larger for politically sensitive topics. \textit{Source:} \citet{goldman2025}.}
\label{fig:polar}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Overconfidence in Private Signals}

The third source is the one emphasized by \citet{daniel1998}. Two agents with the same prior can observe the same public signal and apply different likelihoods because they differ in their confidence about their own private information. The agent with higher confidence in his private signal will place less weight on the public signal and more on his own view; the agent with lower confidence will do the reverse. Both are acting rationally, given their subjective confidence, but they hold heterogeneous beliefs about the asset, and the dispersion in beliefs is larger when the agents are overconfident in their private signals. The mechanism is the bridge from the overconfidence of Section~\ref{sec:overconfidence} to the heterogeneous-beliefs models of the next section.

\section{Heterogeneous Beliefs with Short-Sale Constraints}
\label{sec:hetwithssc}

Heterogeneous beliefs alone do not have interesting pricing effects. If two agents disagree about an asset's value, the optimist wants to buy and the pessimist wants to sell. Without frictions, the two positions can be unwound and the price lies at a weighted average of the two beliefs. The overpricing and underpricing cancel out, and the equilibrium expected return is the rational expected return under the average belief. Heterogeneous beliefs become interesting when pessimists cannot fully express their view---in the canonical case, because short-selling is constrained.

\subsection{Static Argument: Miller (1977)}

\citet{miller1977} observed that if pessimists face binding short-sale constraints, only the optimists' valuation is reflected in the price. The equilibrium price lies above the average belief and above the asset's fundamental value, with the magnitude of the overpricing determined by the dispersion of beliefs. The implication is a sharp empirical prediction: assets that investors disagree about more should be more overpriced, and should earn lower subsequent returns.

The argument is static, in the sense that it does not require beliefs to change over time. It is enough that beliefs differ across agents in the cross-section at a given date, and that the pessimists cannot fully short. The mechanism is also asymmetric across the two legs of the trade: correcting overpricing requires shorting, which is constrained, while correcting underpricing requires only cash. The asymmetry is a deep source of pricing distortion in the heterogeneous-beliefs model.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{34.png}
\caption[Heterogeneous beliefs and overpricing with short-sale constraints]{\textbf{Miller (1977) in pictures.} The figure illustrates the static argument: with short-sale constraints, only the optimist's valuation is reflected in the price, and the equilibrium price exceeds the average belief. The magnitude of the overpricing scales with the dispersion of beliefs. \textit{Source:} EF8083 course materials.}
\label{fig:miller}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Dynamic Argument: Harrison and Kreps (1978)}

The dynamic version of the argument adds a \emph{resale option}. \citet{harrison1978} show that an agent with optimistic beliefs about an asset is willing to pay more than his buy-and-hold valuation, because there is a positive probability that future information will make his view the prevailing one, at which point he can resell to a future optimist at a still higher price. The resale option is valuable precisely because beliefs are heterogeneous: in a homogeneous-belief world there is no one to resell to. With short-sale constraints preventing pessimists from driving the price down, the resale option adds a \emph{bubble component} to the price that can persist even when all agents know the asset is overvalued relative to fundamental value.

The following example, drawn from the \citet{harrison1978} framework, illustrates the magnitude of the resale-option effect.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Worked example.} Two risk-neutral agents $A$ and $B$ trade a risky asset at $t = 0, 1, 2$. The interest rate is zero and short-selling is prohibited. The asset pays 90 in state $u$ and 25 in state $d$ at $t = 2$, and each state has probability $1/2$, so the buy-and-hold (hold-to-maturity) value of the asset is $(90 + 25)/2 = 57.5$. At $t = 1$ a public signal arrives that is only weakly informative about the state, but the two agents interpret it asymmetrically: agent $A$ treats good news as confirmation of state $u$ and values the asset at 90, while remaining at 57.5 after bad news; agent $B$ treats bad news as confirmation of state $d$ and values the asset at 25, while remaining at 57.5 after good news. Good and bad news arrive with probability $1/2$ each. Because short-selling is prohibited, the $t = 1$ price is set by the more optimistic agent: 90 after good news, 57.5 after bad. At $t = 0$, the asset therefore trades at
\[
\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 90 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 57.5 \;=\; 73.75,
\]
which exceeds the buy-and-hold value of 57.5. The excess 16.25 is the resale-option premium: an agent pays more than the asset is worth holding because holding carries the option of reselling to a future optimist. (The example abstracts from discounting.)
\end{quote}

\subsection{Bubble Characteristics}

The bubble component of \citet{harrison1978} has properties that match observed bubble episodes. \citet{scheinkman2003} formalize the result: the magnitude of the resale-option premium is increasing in the volatility of beliefs (the more agents disagree, the larger the option value) and decreasing in the asset float (more shares available to short against means a looser constraint and a smaller bubble). Both predictions are directional: the larger the dispersion of beliefs, the larger the bubble; the more shares available for trading, the smaller the bubble. The predictions are exactly the dimensions on which \citet{xiong2011} take the model to Chinese warrant data (Section~\ref{sec:hetprices}).

\section{Heterogeneous Beliefs and Asset Prices}
\label{sec:hetprices}

\subsection{High Valuations and Heavy Trading}

Models of heterogeneous beliefs with short-sale constraints are popular partly because they explain not just overpricing but also \emph{the coincidence of high valuations and heavy trading}, which is one of the empirical regularities of bubble episodes (Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}). With short-sale constraints, the optimists trade against each other as their beliefs diverge; pessimists who would otherwise trade to express their views are sidelined. The trading volume and the price level both rise, and they do so together. \citet{hong2007} document this co-movement in U.S.\ stock market data.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.40\textwidth]{slide39_pic4.jpg}
\caption[High valuations and trading volume]{\textbf{High valuations and heavy trading.} The figure plots turnover against cumulative returns in the cross-section of U.S.\ stocks. Stocks with the highest turnover also exhibit the highest cumulative returns, consistent with heterogeneous-beliefs models in which optimists trade against each other while pessimists are constrained. \textit{Source:} \citet{hong2007}, reproduced from the original article.}
\label{fig:valtrading}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The Chinese Warrant Bubble}

The most rigorous empirical test of the heterogeneous-beliefs-with-short-sale-constraints theory is \citet{xiong2011} on Chinese warrants. Between 2005 and 2008, more than a dozen put warrants traded on the Chinese exchanges at intrinsic values that were essentially zero (the underlying stocks were deep out of the money relative to exercise prices). Yet the warrants traded at high prices and with enormous turnover. The fundamental value of a deep-out-of-the-money put warrant is very small; the observed prices are an order of magnitude larger.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{slide40_pic3.png}
\caption[Chinese warrant prices and turnover]{\textbf{Chinese warrant bubble.} The figure shows price and turnover for a representative deep-out-of-the-money put warrant traded in China, 2005--2008. Intrinsic value is essentially zero throughout the sample; the warrant trades at high prices and with very high turnover. \textit{Source:} \citet{xiong2011}, reproduced from the original article.}
\label{fig:warrant}
\end{figure}

\citet{xiong2011} document three regularities consistent with the resale-option theory of bubbles. The size of the bubble is increasing in turnover (consistent with high dispersion of beliefs driving both prices and volume); increasing in the volatility of the underlying asset (consistent with the resale-option value rising in the volatility of beliefs); and decreasing in the asset float (consistent with tighter short-sale constraints when fewer shares are available). The warrant data are an unusually clean test because the fundamental value is essentially known and constant, leaving little room for the rational discount-rate counter-story.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{slide41_pic4.png}
\caption[Bubble size and its determinants]{\textbf{Warrant bubble evidence.} The bubble component of warrant prices increases with turnover and volatility and decreases with asset float, consistent with the resale-option theory of speculative bubbles. \textit{Source:} \citet{xiong2011}.}
\label{fig:warrant_determinants}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Cross-Sectional Stock Returns}

The heterogeneous-beliefs model with short-sale constraints also predicts a cross-sectional return pattern: assets that investors disagree about more should be more overpriced, and should earn lower subsequent returns. \citet{diether2003} test the prediction using analyst forecast dispersion as the disagreement measure. They form portfolios sorted on the cross-sectional standard deviation of analyst earnings forecasts and find that high-dispersion stocks earn significantly lower returns than low-dispersion stocks over the subsequent twelve months, even after controlling for size, book-to-market, and momentum. The return spread is consistent with the overpricing mechanism: disagreement is high precisely for stocks where some analysts are optimistic and others are pessimistic, and the short-sale constraint prevents the pessimists from fully expressing their view.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{slide42_pic4.jpg}
\caption[Analyst disagreement and subsequent returns]{\textbf{Cross-sectional disagreement.} The figure shows average returns of portfolios sorted on the cross-sectional dispersion of analyst earnings forecasts. High-dispersion stocks earn lower subsequent returns, consistent with the overpricing prediction of the heterogeneous-beliefs model. \textit{Source:} \citet{diether2003}, reproduced from the original article.}
\label{fig:dms}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Aggregate Stock Market}

The same mechanism predicts a relation at the aggregate level: when aggregate disagreement is high, the market as a whole should be more overpriced and earn lower subsequent returns. \citet{yu2011} measures aggregate disagreement as the value-weighted average of stock-level forecast dispersion and shows that high-dispersion periods are followed by low returns on the aggregate market index.

High-disagreement periods are followed by low market returns, consistent with the heterogeneous-beliefs overpricing mechanism operating at the aggregate level. \textit{Source:} \citet{yu2011}, reproduced from the original article.}

\subsection{Risk--Return Tradeoff}

The same logic produces a prediction about the risk--return relation that goes the wrong way under standard theory. \citet{sharpe1964} and the CAPM that follows from it predict that higher risk is rewarded with higher expected return. In the heterogeneous-beliefs model, the opposite can occur: when investors disagree about a stock, the optimists set the price and the pessimists are sidelined by the short-sale constraint, so higher disagreement is associated with \emph{higher} price and \emph{lower} expected return. The negative risk--return relation is one of the empirical regularities of Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{slide45_pic2.png}
\caption[Risk--return intuition]{\textbf{Risk--return intuition.} The figure illustrates the heterogeneous-beliefs channel: with short-sale constraints, the pessimist is sidelined when investors disagree, and only the optimist's valuation enters the price. Higher disagreement raises the price and lowers the expected return. \textit{Source:} EF8083 course materials.}
\label{fig:riskret_intuition}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Macro Factors and Disagreement}

\citet{li2016} extends the logic from individual stocks to the pricing of macroeconomic factors. If investors disagree about the prospects of consumption growth or inflation, the resulting overpricing should be more pronounced for factor portfolios whose payoffs load more heavily on the most-disputed macro states. The data are consistent with the prediction: factor returns are lower when disagreement about the underlying macro state is higher, and the effect is stronger for factor portfolios more exposed to that state.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\section{Causal Evidence: The EDGAR Identification}
\label{sec:causal}

The cross-sectional and time-series correlations of Section~\ref{sec:hetprices} are consistent with the heterogeneous-beliefs model, but the same correlations admit other readings. A stock with high analyst dispersion is also a stock with high uncertainty, high trading volume, and high media attention; the overpricing interpretation must compete with risk-based and information-based alternatives. Distinguishing the channels requires exogenous variation in disagreement.

\citet{chang2022} provide such variation by exploiting the staggered rollout of the SEC's EDGAR electronic disclosure system. As EDGAR access was extended to subsets of firms during the 1990s, the cost of acquiring public information about those firms fell. The authors argue that lower information costs reduce the dispersion of beliefs: as more investors access the same public information, disagreement about what the firm is worth shrinks. They then use the staggered rollout as a quasi-experiment to identify the effect of disagreement on stock prices.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.30\textwidth]{slide49_pic4.png}
\hspace{0.2em}
\includegraphics[width=0.30\textwidth]{slide49_pic5.png}
\hspace{0.2em}
\includegraphics[width=0.30\textwidth]{slide49_pic6.png}
\caption[EDGAR rollout and disagreement]{\textbf{Causal evidence on disagreement.} The three panels document the EDGAR rollout (left), the associated decline in analyst forecast dispersion (center), and the corresponding change in stock prices (right). Stocks whose information costs fell with EDGAR experienced both lower disagreement and lower prices, consistent with the overpricing mechanism of the heterogeneous-beliefs model. \textit{Source:} \citet{chang2022}, reproduced from the original article.}
\label{fig:chang}
\end{figure}

The central finding is that the rollout of EDGAR, by lowering information costs and disagreement, also lowered stock prices---the sign predicted by the overpricing interpretation. The magnitude is consistent with a quantitatively meaningful share of the variation in prices being driven by disagreement, and the design rules out the alternative interpretations that rely on time-invariant firm characteristics. The result is one of the cleanest causal pieces of evidence in the heterogeneous-beliefs literature, and it sharpens the case for treating disagreement as a priced source of risk in its own right rather than as a proxy for some other dimension of firm fundamentals.

\section{Do People Ever Learn?}
\label{sec:stakes}

The biases of Sections~\ref{sec:representativeness}--\ref{sec:otherbiases} are persistent; they are part of the cognitive and emotional machinery that evolution built into human decision-making. Many of them have survival value (overconfidence increases the willingness to act, which has positive selection consequences), and the kinds of financial decisions in which they matter most are recent on evolutionary timescales. People can, and do, learn to mitigate some biases through training, debiasing, and rule-based decision-making---but the mitigation is partial.

The most direct evidence comes from comparing investor behavior under different stakes. \citet{sui2025} ran a randomized intervention in which investors simultaneously traded stocks in real accounts using their own money and in a simulated setting with no personal financial consequence. The two arms of the study produced sharply different behavior: in the low-stakes simulated setting, investors traded less, diversified more, and earned higher returns. In the high-stakes real setting, the same investors exhibited more of the overtrading, concentration, and overconfidence that the behavioral literature documents.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{slide51_pic4.png}
\caption[Stakes and investor biases]{\textbf{Investors do better when they care less.} In a randomized intervention, investors trading in a low-stakes simulated setting traded less, diversified more, and earned higher returns than the same investors trading in high-stakes real accounts with their own money. The result is consistent with biased beliefs being magnified by the emotional salience of the stakes. \textit{Source:} \citet{sui2025}, reproduced from the original article.}
\label{fig:sui}
\end{figure}

The finding has two implications for the behavioral asset pricing program. First, it provides a clean experimental handle on the role of stakes in producing the patterns the literature documents. The biases are not arbitrary; they are sensitive to the emotional context in which decisions are made, and the emotional context is in turn sensitive to the size of the stake. Second, it raises a methodological point that recurs in the chapter: behavioral biases are not fixed properties of the investor. They are properties of the investor \emph{in context}, and the context matters for how strongly they affect trading and prices.

\section{What Remains Contested}
\label{sec:beliefscontested}

Two strands of evidence keep the behavioral reading honest.

First, the cognitive biases catalogued in Sections~\ref{sec:representativeness}--\ref{sec:otherbiases} are individually well-documented in laboratory data, but their \emph{quantitative} contribution to asset prices is harder to pin down. The disposition effect of Chapter~\ref{ch:preferences} and the trading-cost evidence of Section~\ref{sec:overconfidence} are reasonably direct: investors' biases translate into overtrading and into measurable return patterns. The over-extrapolation evidence is stronger at the survey level than at the trading level: the Greenwood--Shleifer correlations are tight, but the contribution of extrapolative beliefs to the cross-section of stock returns is harder to estimate cleanly. The bias-by-bias literature must be aggregated across biases that may or may not interact, and the empirical case is uneven.

Second, the heterogeneous-beliefs models with short-sale constraints are competitive with rational explanations in several applications. The cross-sectional disagreement result of \citet{diether2003} admits a risk-based interpretation: high-dispersion stocks are also stocks with high fundamental uncertainty, and the low returns may be compensation for that uncertainty rather than the result of overpricing. The warrant-bubble evidence of \citet{xiong2011} is sharper, but it remains a single market; the macro-factor extension of \citet{li2016} is suggestive rather than conclusive. The causal EDGAR evidence of \citet{chang2022} is the strongest single piece of evidence, but it speaks to one channel (information costs and disagreement) rather than to the broader class of bias-driven models.

The methodological lesson is the same as in the earlier chapters: a behavioral model earns credibility when it predicts something not used to fit it, and when that prediction survives out-of-sample tests. The strongest out-of-sample predictions of the biased-beliefs program are the disposition effect and the trading-cost evidence of overconfidence, the Greenwood--Shleifer correlations of survey expectations and returns, the warrant-bubble evidence on resale-option values, and the EDGAR causal evidence on disagreement. The rest of the literature is competitive with rational alternatives.

\section{The Road Ahead}
\label{sec:beliefsahead}

This chapter has developed the second of the two departures from the rational benchmark---biased beliefs---together with the cross-sectional dispersion in beliefs that the heterogeneous-beliefs model exploits. Combined with the prospect-theory departures of Chapter~\ref{ch:preferences}, the book now has the two psychological ingredients needed for most of behavioral finance: a non-standard theory of preferences under risk, and a non-standard theory of belief formation. The combination is what most applications in the chapters that follow deploy.

The remaining chapters assemble these ingredients into specific empirical and theoretical applications. Chapter~\ref{ch:bounded} develops \emph{bounded rationality}: limited attention, category-based thinking, and psychology-free models of noise trading and sentiment. A further chapter develops \emph{memory-based} approaches---the way investors form beliefs from the cases they remember rather than from a Bayesian aggregation of evidence. These models retain rational beliefs in a thin sense but relax the assumption that all available information is processed.

Together with Chapter~\ref{ch:intro} on the empirical facts, Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} on limits to arbitrage, Chapter~\ref{ch:preferences} on preferences, and the present chapter on biased beliefs, the book develops the building blocks of behavioral asset pricing. The remaining chapters apply them to specific settings---the role of attention, the formation of investor sentiment, and the pricing of memory-driven beliefs---in each case tracing the path from a documented cognitive bias to a measurable asset-pricing implication.
